The architecture: build from the bottom up

Each layer rests on the one below. Skipping ahead is how people lose money. The Start page tracks these steps for you.

1
Cashflow: earn more than you spend

Nothing above works without a gap between income and spending. Saving 10 to 20% of income is a common target.

2
Safety net: 3 to 6 months of spending

Kept in instant-access savings, not invested. Its job is to be there when income stops, not to grow.

3
Expensive debt: pay it off first

Clearing a 15% debt is a guaranteed 15% return, which no investment can promise.

4
Protection: insure against ruin

Health cover, personal liability, and income protection if others depend on you.

5
Core growth: broad, low-cost index funds

Global stock ETFs bought on a schedule. For long-term money this is usually the largest piece.

6
Stability: bonds and savings

Sized by when you need the money. The closer the date, the larger this layer.

7
Satellites: single stocks, themes, crypto

Only money you can afford to lose. A common guideline is 5 to 10% of your investments at most.

The ideas underneath

Compounding

Interest earns interest. €100 a month at 7% a year for 30 years grows to about €122,000, of which only €36,000 is your own money. Starting early matters more than starting big.

Inflation

Prices rise over time, so idle money buys less each year. At 3% inflation, buying power halves in about 24 years (72 divided by 3).

Risk and return

Higher expected return comes with bigger swings. Stocks can fall 30% or more in a bad year and still have rewarded patient holders over decades.

Diversification

Spread money across many companies, countries and asset types so one failure cannot ruin you. One global index ETF holds thousands of companies.

Fees

A 1% yearly fee sounds small. Over 30 years at 7% gross it cuts the final value by roughly a quarter. Check the TER (total expense ratio) of any fund.

Stocks, bonds and cash

Stocks are ownership: highest growth, biggest swings. Bonds are loans: steadier income and a cushion when stocks fall. Cash is safe but loses ground to inflation.

Words you will meet

ETF

Exchange-traded fund: a basket of many investments you buy as one share. A world ETF holds thousands of companies.

Index

A list of companies used as a yardstick, such as the S&P 500 or the DAX. An index fund simply copies it.

Yield

The yearly income an investment pays, as a percentage of its price.

Dividend

A share of profit a company pays its shareholders, usually once or twice a year.

Net worth

Everything you own minus everything you owe.

Savings rate

The share of your income left after spending. Higher means faster progress.

Bonds

A bond is a loan you make. The borrower, a government or company, pays you interest and returns your money on a set date. Bonds are the steady part of a portfolio. Live bond yields and top performing bond funds are on the Research page.

The one idea that explains bonds

Prices and yields move opposite

When rates rise, older lower-paying bonds lose value, and when rates fall they gain. Held to maturity you still get face value back, so a dip only hurts if you must sell early.

Government bondsLow risk

Lowest yields, highest safety

Issued by national governments such as German Bunds, US Treasuries or UK gilts. Watch out: weaker governments carry real default risk, and local-currency bonds lose value if inflation bites.

Corporate bondsLow to medium

Yield rises as credit quality falls

Loans to companies. Investment grade (BBB and above) is sturdier. Watch out: the company can default.

High-yield (junk) bondsHigh risk

Pays more because it can fail

Companies rated below BBB. In a downturn they behave more like stocks than bonds.

Inflation-linked bondsLow risk

Principal rises with inflation

Protect buying power over decades, but can still fall in price when real yields rise.

Zero-coupon bondsRate sensitive

Bought at a discount, paid at par

No interest payments. Good for a known sum on a known date, but the longest ones swing hardest.

Bond funds and ETFsDiversified

One purchase, hundreds of bonds

The easiest way for most people to own bonds. A fund never matures, so its price can stay down after rates rise.

Bond vocabulary

Par, coupon, maturity

Par is the amount repaid. The coupon is the fixed yearly interest. Maturity is the repayment date.

Current yield versus yield to maturity

Current yield is coupon over price. Yield to maturity also counts the gain or loss against par, so use it to compare bonds.

Credit ratings

Agencies grade borrowers from AAA (strongest) to D (in default). BBB- and above is investment grade.

Duration

Sensitivity to interest rates. A duration of 7 means roughly a 7% price fall if rates rise 1 point.

Bond ladders

Split money across bonds maturing in different years. One matures regularly and you reinvest at current rates.

Main risks

Interest rate, credit, inflation, call and liquidity risk.

Your research checklist

Before buying a stock

What does it sell and to whom? Is revenue growing? Is it profitable and how much debt does it carry? What is the valuation (price to earnings) against peers? Why is the price moving? How much of your money would this be?

Before buying a bond

Who is the borrower and what is the rating? What is the yield to maturity? When does it mature? What is the duration? Which currency? Can you sell it easily?

Before buying an ETF

What index does it track? Total expense ratio? Is it EU-registered (UCITS)? Accumulating or distributing? Fund size, and how closely it tracks its index?

How it fits together

Where each layer lives

Safety net: instant-access savings (Tagesgeld), covered by the EU deposit guarantee of €100,000 per person per bank. Investments: a securities account (Depot) at a regulated broker that handles German tax reporting.

Use your allowance

File a Freistellungsauftrag with each bank or broker so the first €1,000 of income is paid out untaxed. Split it across providers if you have several.

ETFs: UCITS, accumulating or distributing

US-listed ETFs are generally not offered to EU retail investors, so look for UCITS ETFs. Accumulating funds reinvest income but can trigger the Vorabpauschale. Distributing funds pay cash that is taxed when received.

German bonds

Federal bonds (Bunds) are the benchmark safe asset, from 2 to 30 years. Interest is taxed at 26.375% after your allowance with no partial exemption.

Pensions

State-supported products exist, including the employer scheme (bAV), Riester and Rürup. Rules keep changing, so check current terms.

Sources worth trusting

Bundesbank for yields, BaFin for consumer protection, Finanztip and Stiftung Warentest for independent guides, and a Steuerberater for personal tax.
