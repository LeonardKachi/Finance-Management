How money works

Read this first. It explains the ideas every other tab depends on, then the order to build your finances in. It is general education, not advice.

The architecture: build from the bottom up

Each layer rests on the one below. Skipping ahead is how people lose money.

1
Cashflow: earn more than you spend

Nothing above works without a gap between income and spending. Track it in Cashflow. Saving 10 to 20% of income is a common target.

2
Safety net: 3 to 6 months of spending

Kept in instant-access savings, not invested. Its job is to be there when your income stops, not to grow.

3
Expensive debt: pay it off first

Clearing a 15% credit or overdraft debt is a guaranteed 15% return, which no investment can promise.

4
Protection: insure against ruin

Health cover, personal liability, and income protection if others depend on you. Cheap relative to the disasters they cover.

5
Core growth: broad, low-cost index funds

Global stock ETFs bought on a schedule. For long-term money this is usually the largest piece. Research here means choosing a cheap, diversified fund, not picking winners.

6
Stability: bonds and savings

Sized by when you need the money. The closer the date, the larger this layer. Use the planner below.

7
Satellites: single stocks, themes, crypto

Only money you can afford to lose. A common guideline is 5 to 10% of your investments, at most.

The ideas underneath

Compounding

Interest earns interest. €100 a month at 7% a year for 30 years grows to about €122,000, of which only €36,000 is money you put in. Time does most of the work, so starting early matters more than starting big.

Inflation

Prices rise over time, so money sitting idle buys less each year. At 3% inflation, buying power halves in about 24 years (72 divided by 3). Your savings need to earn at least the inflation rate to stand still.

Risk and return

Higher expected return comes with bigger swings. Stocks can fall 30% or more in a bad year and have still rewarded patient holders over decades. The swings are the price of the return, not a malfunction.

Diversification

Spread money across many companies, countries and asset types so one failure cannot ruin you. A single global index ETF holds thousands of companies at once.

Fees

A 1% yearly fee sounds small. Over 30 years at 7% gross it cuts the final value by roughly a quarter. Check the TER (total expense ratio) before buying any fund.

Time horizon

Money you need within about five years should not depend on the stock market. Money you will not touch for 15 years can ride out the swings.

Stocks, bonds and cash

Stocks are ownership: highest growth, biggest swings. Bonds are loans: steadier income and a cushion when stocks fall. Cash is safe and liquid but loses ground to inflation. A sound plan mixes them for your horizon.

Your research checklist

Before buying a stock

What does it sell and to whom? Is revenue growing? Is it profitable and how much debt does it carry? What is the valuation (price to earnings) against peers? Why is the price moving? How much of your money would this be? Write the thesis in one sentence.

Before buying a bond

Who is the borrower and what is the credit rating? What is the yield to maturity? When does it mature? What is the duration (rate sensitivity)? Which currency? Can you sell it easily?

Before buying an ETF

What index does it track and how many holdings? Total expense ratio? Is it EU-registered (UCITS)? Accumulating or distributing? Fund size, and how closely it tracks its index?

How it fits together

Where each layer lives

Safety net: instant-access savings (Tagesgeld) at a bank, covered by the EU deposit guarantee of €100,000 per person per bank. Investments: a securities account (Depot) at a regulated broker, ideally one that handles German tax reporting for you.

Use your allowance

File a Freistellungsauftrag with each bank or broker so the first €1,000 of income is paid out untaxed. Split it across providers if you have several, and never exceed the total.

ETFs: UCITS, accumulating or distributing

US-listed ETFs are generally not offered to EU retail investors, so look for UCITS ETFs. Accumulating funds reinvest income but can trigger the Vorabpauschale, a small yearly tax. Distributing funds pay cash that is taxed when received.

German bonds

Federal bonds (Bunds) are the benchmark safe asset, with maturities from 2 to 30 years. Interest is taxed at 26.375% after your allowance, with no partial exemption, which is why bond funds look worse after tax than stock funds.

Pensions

State-supported products exist, including the employer scheme (bAV), Riester and Rürup. Rules and reforms keep changing, so check the current terms before committing.

Sources worth trusting

Bundesbank for yields and statistics, BaFin for consumer protection, Finanztip and Stiftung Warentest for independent guides, and a Steuerberater for your personal tax. Compare at least two before acting.

How bonds work

Prices and yields move opposite

When rates rise, older lower-paying bonds lose value, and when rates fall they gain. Held to maturity you still get face value back, so a dip only hurts if you must sell early.

Par, coupon, maturity

Par is the amount repaid. The coupon is the yearly interest as a share of par. Maturity is the repayment date.

Current yield versus yield to maturity

Current yield is coupon over price. Yield to maturity also counts the gain or loss against par, so use it to compare bonds.

Types

Government: safest, lowest yield. Corporate: more income, can default. High-yield: pays more because it can fail. Inflation-linked: principal follows inflation. Zero-coupon: no interest, bought at a discount. Savings bonds and treasury bills: retail products, compare their rate with local inflation.

Risks and ladders

Rate, credit, inflation, call and liquidity risk are the main ones. A ladder splits money across bonds maturing in different years, so one matures regularly and you reinvest at current rates.
